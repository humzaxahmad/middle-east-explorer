trigger: "The US and Israel struck Iran on 28 February 2026, opening a war that has now run for more than eight months. Iran responded by declaring a restricted shipping zone across the Strait of Hormuz and attacking tankers that tried to cross it, the narrow passage that around 20 million barrels of oil a day moved through before the war."
energyHit: "Hormuz carries roughly 35% of the world's seaborne crude oil trade, and the initial disruption cut close to 10 million barrels a day from global supply, the largest oil supply shock on record. Traffic has partly recovered since, with the US saying it is escorting tankers through, but the strait remains a contested, heavily impaired route rather than a closed or a normal one: Kpler tracking put crossings at about 11.9 million barrels a day in early October 2026, still roughly 30% below the pre-war level, and tanker attacks hit their highest weekly count of the year on 9 October. War-risk insurance for a transit has swung between about 2% and 10% of a ship's hull value over the course of the war, against roughly 0.25% before it; at the high end, insuring a single transit for a $100 million tanker now costs $3 million to $10 million. Brent crude peaked near $118 a barrel in early March 2026 and traded near $116 in late October. The war has also reached beyond shipping lanes directly: an Iranian strike on Qatar's Ras Laffan complex in March 2026 destroyed 17% of its LNG capacity outright, a direct physical loss on top of the insurance and transit problem."
economyHit: "The World Bank's April 2026 outlook projected energy prices up 24% for the year, the sharpest jump since Russia's invasion of Ukraine in 2022. US inflation reached 3.8% year-on-year by April 2026, its highest since May 2023, and the Federal Reserve held rates at 3.50%-3.75% rather than cutting, with markets pricing a 20-30% chance of a hike instead; the European Central Bank moved away from its planned rate cuts too. For Gulf producers the damage shows up in growth, not just prices: the IMF's October 2026 outlook expects the six GCC economies to contract 4.8% as a bloc in 2026, with Kuwait (-16.7%) and Qatar (-15.6%) hit hardest and Oman the only one to grow (+4.1%), before a sharp rebound (+9.1% for the bloc) once the IMF's assumed phased reopening begins in March 2027."
sectorsWinners:
  - "Tankers: owners still willing to sail can charge freight rates that price in the war-risk premium"
  - "Gulf producers with spare bypass-pipeline capacity, chiefly Saudi Arabia and the UAE, who can keep selling oil that never has to cross the strait"
  - "Oilfield services and pipeline contractors building the new bypass capacity"
sectorsLosers:
  - "Airlines, especially Gulf carriers rerouting around the conflict zone and absorbing higher fuel costs"
  - "Energy-intensive industry: chemicals, heavy industry and autos, facing higher input costs"
  - "Households and retailers in energy-importing countries, squeezed by higher inflation and, in the US and eurozone, central banks in no hurry to cut rates"

whatToWatch: "The IMF assumes a gradual reopening from March 2027 and a return to pre-war shipping conditions by October 2027; both dates depend on the war actually winding down. Three things worth tracking: the weekly war-risk insurance rate, a real-time price on how dangerous insurers think the strait is; Kpler's daily tanker-transit count against the roughly 20 million barrel a day pre-war baseline; and whether the UAE's second bypass pipeline (about half built as of May 2026, targeting 3.6 million barrels a day by 2027) and Kuwait's early-stage bypass talks with Saudi Arabia and the UAE actually reach completion."
interviewAngle: "Don't just say \"higher oil prices help oil producers.\" Kuwait and Qatar, the two Gulf states with no real way around the strait, posted the worst 2026 growth forecasts in the entire GCC, while Oman, which exports from the Gulf of Oman rather than through Hormuz, was the only Gulf economy to grow. Within the same shock, geography and bypass infrastructure mattered more than simply having oil."
summary: "Iran's restriction of the Strait of Hormuz since the February 2026 war with the US and Israel has cut tanker traffic by about 30%, pushed Brent crude above $100 a barrel for most of the year, and driven a sharp divide within the Gulf: producers with bypass pipelines took a smaller hit, Oman (which doesn't need one) grew, and Kuwait and Qatar, which have none, posted the region's worst 2026 growth forecasts."
