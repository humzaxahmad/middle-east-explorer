title: "Peak oil demand and the Gulf"
speed: slow
trigger: "There's no single trigger: it's a structural question about when, or whether, global oil demand peaks and starts to decline for good, as electric vehicles, efficiency gains and a slowing China erode the main source of demand growth. The IEA and OPEC now disagree sharply even about 2026 itself, not just the long run."
energyHit: "The IEA's October 2026 outlook has global oil demand falling 2.6 million barrels a day in 2026, to 102.4 million b/d, driven mainly by a China slowdown and faster EV adoption; OPEC's outlook has demand still growing 0.2 million b/d, to 105.7 million b/d, over the same year. The gap is concentrated almost entirely in non-OECD demand, where OPEC expects growth of 0.7 million b/d and the IEA expects a 0.6 million b/d contraction. On the IEA's longer framing, global demand plateaus near 105.6 million b/d around 2029 before declining; OPEC's position, stated flatly by its Secretary General, is that no peak is in sight during its forecast period at all. Electric vehicles are the clearest swing factor: global EV sales reached 20.7 million in 2025, up 20% year on year and 22.6% of all cars sold, with China alone accounting for 63% of EV sales and Europe's EV market growing 33% in the same year."
economyHit: "For oil exporters, a demand peak isn't a crash, it's a slower-moving version of the same budget pressure: less certain long-run revenue growth to fund today's diversification plans and public spending, even before the 2026 Hormuz shock already showed how exposed Gulf budgets still are to energy income in the near term. Gulf sovereign wealth funds, which collectively hold more than $6 trillion (Saudi Arabia's PIF over $700bn, Abu Dhabi's ADIA over $1 trillion), are the region's main hedge: they exist explicitly to convert today's oil wealth into assets that keep paying out after oil demand peaks. The IMF has previously estimated that, without further fiscal reform, some GCC states could exhaust their financial buffers by the mid-2030s."
sectorsWinners:
  - "Renewables and battery/EV supply chains, which gain demand every year oil doesn't"
  - "Gulf sovereign wealth funds' non-oil holdings, the explicit hedge against this exact scenario"
  - "Countries and companies that got ahead on diversification before the peak arrives, rather than after"
sectorsLosers:
  - "Oil-dependent budgets with high fiscal breakeven prices and little non-oil revenue, which have the least room to absorb slower long-run demand growth"
  - "Oilfield services firms geared toward long-cycle, high-cost projects that need decades of demand growth to pay back"
  - "Any producer still planning capacity expansions on the assumption that 2010s-style demand growth continues"
countriesExposed: ["IRQ", "KWT"]
countriesProtected: ["ARE", "SAU"]
whatToWatch: "Whether the IEA and OPEC's 2026 forecasts converge or diverge further as the year closes, since a widening gap signals even more uncertainty for producers planning budgets around a specific price and volume; China's EV sales share, the single biggest swing factor in non-OECD demand; and whether Gulf sovereign wealth funds' non-oil returns are large enough to offset slower oil revenue growth, or whether states draw down reserves instead, as the IMF's depletion warnings assume."
interviewAngle: "If asked whether peak oil demand is a real risk or a talking point, point to the forecast gap itself: the IEA and OPEC disagree by more than 3 million barrels a day for 2026 alone, not some distant future year. Two of the industry's most-watched forecasters can't agree on what's happening right now, which should make any single number in a Gulf budget plan look less certain than it's presented."
summary: "Global oil demand forecasts for 2026 itself now diverge by more than 3 million barrels a day between the IEA (demand falling to 102.4 million b/d) and OPEC (demand rising to 105.7 million b/d), driven mainly by how fast electric vehicles and China's economy are changing. Gulf states with the least diversification and the highest fiscal breakeven prices, like Iraq and Kuwait, carry the most long-run exposure to a real peak; those furthest along in building a non-oil economy and sovereign wealth buffer, like the UAE and Saudi Arabia, are better positioned, though not immune."
